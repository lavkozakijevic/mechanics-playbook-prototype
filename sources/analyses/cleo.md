## Overview

Cleo is a US personal finance app that the opening screen presents as a "save money and build credit app". It connects to the user's bank account through Plaid and delivers most of its functions through a chat interface with a named AI persona, alongside a nav bar with spend, budget, chat, save, borrow and, after onboarding completes, habits. In this session the app primarily asked the user to set and stay inside a monthly spending limit, and to come back to review, rate and categorize their own transactions.

## First impression

The launch screen carries four slides that the user swipes through: up to $500 in cash advances "when you need a little extra"; "learn to spend better with spending limits and budget tips"; "stress less about your score, apply for the credit builder card"; and "simplify your savings with automatic transfers to a savings wallet". Sign up and "I already have an account" buttons sit at the bottom.

Sign up asks, one screen at a time: full legal name (first, last), email, a date of birth, which US state the user lives in, where the user heard about Cleo (app store, play store, Facebook, friend, family and other options), then email verification with a code, a resend option, a "sent to a different email" option and an "open my email" button. After the code is entered the app responds "that totally worked". Nothing about the user's money is shown before this point.

A screen then states that due to the laws of the user's state, cash advances on certain subscriptions such as plus or builder are not available, while "insights, budget and savings tools are good to go", presented in chat or SMS style bubbles. Next is a notifications ask, "never miss a dollar", with a sample notification reading "you smashed yet another target. Stick to $50 today to stay on track", three bullets (tailored coaching on your spending, watch Cleo grow your savings, reminders that you're only human, with a blue heart), and "not now" or "turn on notifications". Next is the Plaid connection screen, which states that Plaid connects Cleo securely, that the user is "in good company with millions of users", that Cleo cannot move the user's money and never stores bank login details, with buttons "connect me" and "I need more info and security". The Plaid sheet then explains connecting effortlessly, data ownership, and fraud and risk protection before phone number, institution, credentials and an identity verification code. An "are you ready?" screen follows, with the button "unleash cleo".

The first mechanic moment is the chat that opens immediately after that button. Message bubbles load, the app greets the user by name, and offers four options: control my spending, get a cash advance, improve my credit score, and "I don't know, see where my money goes". Choosing "control my spending" starts a five question set about money habits ("be honest in the next five questions"), including how much of income was usually spent over the last six months (options: not much; most of it; about the same; all of it and then some; all of it and then a lot more) and "what are you like with bills?" (options: I'm on top of them; they're on top of me). It ends with a "roast me" button.

## Core activity

The experience runs as a conversation with a persona that comments on the user's own bank data in a joking register, wrapped around a budgeting tool. Inside the app the user connects accounts and sets a monthly spending limit and optional category limits, confirms which transactions are bills and swipes to keep or ditch subscriptions, reads chat cards about balance, upcoming bills and affordability, categorizes uncategorized transactions when asked, plays an in chat quiz about their own money, and rates past purchases as loved or regretted in scheduled spending reviews.

## Mechanics observed

### Challenge · Core · confirmed

**What was observed:**

Three surfaces in this session create bounded objectives whose outcome the app tracks.

1. The monthly spending limit. After onboarding, chat bubbles state that the app will lay out what is coming in and out, that it gives "a daily spending limit", and that the user checks their phone to stay on track, followed by "you're all set, a spend limit a day keeps the overdraft away, think of it as a harness there to protect you from financial chaos". The budget tab shows the current month, how many days are left until the end of the month, two coin stacks for money in and money out, the limit, how much has been spent, and what is due to be spent. Tapping the limit opens "set your limit for the month", showing an amount "including all bills" and a category limits toggle. With category limits on, the app lists transfer out, general, bank charges, transport, cash, bills, savings, shopping, holidays, groceries, treatment, eating out, loans and expenses, each with plus and minus controls for setting a limit, under the line "based on your average spending", and a confirm button reading "all right I can do this". The user can switch months by tapping the date. While spending is inside the limit a thumbs up emoji appears under the two coin stacks. When it has been exceeded, a skull appears, the line beneath the month turns red, the copy reads "rip budget", and a past month is summarized with the amount and the word "overspent" beneath it.

2. The in chat game. A chat card described as "oh it's game time, I think you know your money, play the game with self respect" carries three buttons: play game, stats, opt out. Playing opens a new chat that asks "did you pay any bills this week?" with yes and no options, and returns "sadly not correct, you can add more here" with a route to add bills. The next question asks how much money the user has "sunk" in the last seven days, with three amounts to choose from; a wrong choice returns "nope, we're looking", then the correct amount, then "next question". Several questions follow. The stats screen shows "knowledge score 0%", times played once, times won zero, streak one, and a game FAQ. The narrator's account of why the first answer was marked incorrect ("it knows that we didn't add bills and we didn't pay bills through cleo") is his own reading, not something the transcript shows the app stating.

3. The vice challenge in the habits tab. The tab opens with "turn vices into savings, cut your worst spending habits and finally start saving", a "21 of 21 on track" state, and a widget headed "eating out limits" showing an amount on the right and a progress bar, with the line "you're nailing this challenge, stay on track to save 85". The narrator states that this displayed state is a mockup of what it would look like and is not active for this account. Tapping start opens "start challenges and save money, control impulse spending and achieve your savings goals" with three items: "challenge a vice, pick your worst spending habit and put your willpower to the test"; "start saving, keep track of challenges and watch your savings grow"; and "spot trends, review your spending to expose your vices and gain saving tips". The session did not start a vice challenge; tapping through led to the spending reviews screen instead.

**How it is presented:**

The monthly limit is reached from the budget tab and is also restated in the chat as a "stay on top" card reading "you're good, 1200 left of your 1400 budget ending on a date", with income, bills and budget buttons. The on track and overspent states are carried by emoji (thumbs up, skull), a colour change on the line under the month, and short copy. The game is presented as one card among the swipeable chat cards rather than as a separate destination. The vice challenge is the headline of its own nav tab.

**Classification basis:**

Exclusions checked first. The monthly limit is not a target presented without an instance: the app maintains a per month state that can be inspected for earlier months, and it is not merely a scheduled task becoming due. It is not a retrospective record of an accomplishment, since the limit is set before the month runs. It is not classified from label or graphic; the behaviour is evidenced by the differing system responses. The required conditions are then satisfied: a discrete instance (the month), an observable success condition (spending at or below the set limit, with category level limits available), an end boundary (the end of the month, with days remaining displayed), a maintained concluded state (a past month showing the skull and "overspent"), and a different system response for the two outcomes (thumbs up versus skull, red line and "rip budget"). This matches the constraint challenge variant. The game round satisfies the same invariant within one session: defined questions with correct and incorrect responses and a maintained result state ("times won"), matching the session challenge variant. The vice challenge is described by the product with a success condition, an amount to save and a day count, but the session did not instantiate one, and the on track widget was identified by the narrator as a mockup, so that third surface is plausible rather than confirmed and its operating rules are not observed.

**What is notable:**

The success condition of the game is the user's knowledge of their own recorded financial facts, checked against the data the app holds, rather than performance at a task inside the app. The narrator called this "a very interesting way of creating a GameFi experience with a quiz related to payments within the app".

**Screenshot suggestions:**

- The budget tab in the on track state, showing the month, days remaining, the two coin stacks and the thumbs up emoji. Evidences the active instance and its success state.
- A month in the overspent state, showing the skull, the red line under the month and the word "overspent" with the amount. Evidences the concluded non completed outcome.
- The "set your limit for the month" sheet with category limits switched on. Evidences the defined success condition and its scope.
- The game stats screen showing knowledge score, times played, times won and streak. Evidences the tracked result state of the game rounds.

### Daily / Weekly Quests · Supporting · plausible

**What was observed:**

The habits tab, after the start screen, shows "habits", "you have spending reviews", "review streak", a count of zero at that point, and "for review 20 spends", with three items: a review that can be started now with the buttons "start review" and "rate your spends", another review coming tomorrow, and another review in three days. Starting the review opens a practice round: "rate your spends, swipe right or hit loved if it was a purchase you don't regret", with a stack of cards, a "regret" option and a "don't know" option, and a one of three counter, ending with "that's all, you're set. The more you play, the more trends you'll spot so I can help you hit your goals". The real round then runs through the user's own payments with loved, regret and don't know choices, and ends with "you're done for today", a summary screen reading "ratings are in" with counts of regrets and loved items and the total amounts for each, and a "don't know" group. After completion, the first item is marked done, the review streak reads one, the next review is stated as due tomorrow, and the copy reads "play again tomorrow to remain a spending review legend". Below the reviews the app shows "tips and truths" tied to specific transactions, one referencing a McDonald's spend with an amount and the line "you could have been another coffee", and one about account fees with an amount.

**How it is presented:**

The reviews are listed as a set of dated items inside the habits tab, with the next two shown before they are available. The rating interaction is card swiping, preceded by a practice round of three cards.

**Classification basis:**

Exclusions checked first. Access to the app is not the qualifying condition, so this is not daily login rewards. It is not a standing goal: the app releases separate review instances tied to dates and marks the current one done. It is not a retrospective reward, and it is not a recommendation without a tracked state, since the completed review flips to done and increments the review streak. What keeps this at plausible rather than confirmed is the cadence condition: the observed schedule was one review available now, one tomorrow and one in three days, plus the copy "play again tomorrow", which establishes recurring issuance but does not establish a daily or weekly boundary as the entry requires. The observation that would resolve it is the issuance rule across a full week, specifically whether a review is released every day or on a variable schedule. Challenge was checked against this surface as the sibling label: the session does not show a boundary after which an unstarted review expires or closes, so the challenge boundary condition is not met on this evidence; if reviews do expire, both labels would apply, as the Challenge entry's quest note allows.

**Screenshot suggestions:**

- The habits list showing the review streak, "for review 20 spends", the startable review and the two scheduled ones. Evidences recurring issuance and per instance state.
- The completed state showing the first review marked done, the streak at one and "play again tomorrow to remain a spending review legend". Evidences the completion state belonging to the instance.
- The "ratings are in" summary with the loved and regret counts and amounts. Evidences the defined completion condition of one instance.

### Streak · Supporting · confirmed

**What was observed:**

Two streak states appear. In the habits tab the app displays "review streak" at zero before any review is completed, and the same field reads one after the first review is completed, alongside the copy "play again tomorrow to remain a spending review legend". In the game stats screen the app displays "streak one" next to knowledge score 0%, times played once and times won zero.

**How it is presented:**

Both are numeric fields inside their own surfaces: the review streak sits at the top of the habits list next to the reviews it counts, and the game streak sits in the game stats screen alongside the other three counters.

**Classification basis:**

Presence is asserted under the operating card's presence standard: the app labels both fields as streaks, and the review streak was observed changing from zero to one in response to a completed review, which is a recognizable implementation and the product's own framing. The Streak entry's exclusion that directs an unresolved marking when the evidence does not show whether gaps matter is treated here as behaviour detail rather than presence, per the operating card, which states that entry clauses requiring observation of missed period effects apply to describing the mechanic rather than to asserting it. Accordingly the continuity rule is recorded as not observed: the session does not show what a missed review or a missed game does to either number, whether a grace period or protection exists, or what period each streak counts. The game streak is additionally ambiguous on its own terms, since it reads one while times won reads zero, and the transcript does not show what the game streak counts. That ambiguity is recorded, not resolved.

**Screenshot suggestions:**

- The habits header with the review streak at zero, captured before the first review. Evidences the initial continuity state.
- The same header with the streak at one after the review is completed. Evidences the state responding to a qualifying occurrence.
- The game stats screen showing streak alongside times played and times won. Evidences the second streak surface and the ambiguity between the two fields.

## Behavioral interpretation

This section interprets the observational record above. It is separate from that record, and nothing here is evidence of effectiveness.

### Patterns instantiated

- **Bounded Completable Goals** (strongly supported). The monthly limit, the game round and the described vice challenge each define a finite objective whose completion the app can distinguish from non completion. Evidence: the set limit with a month boundary and the thumbs up versus skull outcomes; the game's correct and incorrect responses and times won counter.
- **Visible Progress** (strongly supported). The app converts spending into representations that change: the two coin stacks, the limit against the amount spent and the amount due, days remaining in the month, the per category limits, the progress bar on the eating out limit widget, and the review list moving to done. Evidence is in the budget tab and habits tab observations.
- **Immediate Feedback** (strongly supported). Qualifying actions produce an observable response at the moment they occur: the review streak moving from zero to one on completing a review, the "done" marker, the "sadly not correct" and "nope" responses in the game, the check mark appearing on a completed chat card, and the thumbs up or skull attached to the current budget state.
- **Scheduled Return Triggers** (strongly supported). The app attaches value to recurring windows: reviews dated for tomorrow and in three days, "play again tomorrow", a daily spending limit, a chat card reading "good to check every day", and the notification opt in with a sample reading "stick to $50 today to stay on track".
- **Continuity Stakes** (plausible). Two streak states are maintained. The pattern's invariant requires that the same occurrences produce a different state depending on whether an interruption occurs, and the session does not show what a missed review or missed game does, so the classification rests on the app's own framing rather than on observed differential behaviour. Alternative reading: the counters record how many consecutive items have been completed so far without any state consequence attached to a gap. The observation that would separate them is a session that skips a scheduled review and shows the streak afterwards.
- **Contextual Education** (strongly supported for the connection and bills surfaces). Explanations appear at the decision point rather than as detached reference: the Plaid screen explains what Plaid does and what Cleo cannot do immediately before the bank connection step; the bill confirmation popup defines what a bill is at the moment the user is asked to confirm bills; the plan comparison offers "compare the plans in checklist form because all brains work in different ways" and an FAQ at the point of choosing a plan. The gambling addiction article is a separate case: the narrator could not tell why it appeared, so whether it is attached to a decision state is not observed.
- **Continuity Stakes, conditions absent case** (plausible). The pattern entry notes that when continuity is applied to a behaviour that does not naturally recur at the tracked period, the state predominantly records interruption. The reviews are scheduled a day and three days apart and the game has no observed cadence, and the session gives no evidence about how often users actually complete either. Recorded as an observation about the design's context, not as an outcome claim. Alternative reading: the review cadence is deliberately spaced precisely because daily review is not expected.

### Obstacles the design appears to address

These are hypotheses about the product's apparent aims, not claims about users.

- **No Reason to Return Today** (plausible). The app creates period specific reasons to open it: reviews dated for specific days, "play again tomorrow", a daily spending limit, "good to check every day", and a notification ask presented before any of the app's own data is shown. Alternative reading: the scheduled reviews exist to collect user labels on transactions, which the app also solicits directly through "make Cleo smarter", and the return cadence is a by product of the labelling work rather than its aim. What would separate them: whether reviews continue to be issued once the user's transactions are fully categorized.
- **Value Is Not Understood** (plausible). Explanation is attached to the points where a user might not know what will happen: what Plaid does and what the app cannot do, what counts as a bill, what each plan includes, an FAQ, testimonials, and a plain statement of the state law restriction before the user reaches the paid features. Alternative reading: these are compliance and trust surfaces required for a financial product, and their presence says nothing about a diagnosed comprehension gap.
- **The Behavior Is Boring** (plausible). Budgeting activity is wrapped in a joking persona, gifs, emoji, large type, a quiz game, a roast and a hype mode. Alternative reading, and the one the transcript supports more directly: this is an audience positioning choice. The narrator described the copy as "Gen Z language" and "modernized", which is a statement about voice rather than about a diagnosed obstacle. No evidence in the session bears on whether users experience the underlying activity as under stimulating.
- **Progress Is Invisible** (weakly supported). The app's central promise, stated in chat as laying out what is coming in and out, is to make spending legible. Alternative reading, and the stronger one: this is the product's core utility rather than a remedy for an obstacle, so the label adds nothing the observational record does not already say.

### Principles

Calibrated language only. Product evidence can establish a decision structure; it cannot establish that any user responded to it.

- **Commitment and Consistency** (plausible). The app creates conditions consistent with this principle: the user actively answers five questions about their own habits, sets a monthly limit and optional per category limits, and confirms through buttons phrased as personal declarations, and the app later restates the chosen limit back to the user in chat cards. Alternative: the limit is simply the parameter the tool needs to function.
- **Loss Aversion** (plausible). The app establishes a reference state that can be moved below: a set limit, an amount remaining, days left in the month, and a distinct overspent state carrying a skull and the words "rip budget". Presence of that structure is not evidence that any user experienced the state as a loss, and the layer one entry explicitly rules out inferring the principle from a streak or a warning alone.
- **Competence and Mastery Need** (plausible). The app creates conditions consistent with this principle through feedback that is interpretable against the user's own prior state: a knowledge score with times played and times won, "the more you play, the more trends you'll spot", and a categorization flow framed as making the app smarter. Alternative: the score is a scoring device for a quiz and carries no observed progression.
- **Default and Friction Sensitivity** (plausible). Several paths are shortened or pre resolved for the user: detected income sources offered for selection instead of manual entry, suggested bills drawn from the connected account, suggested categories in the categorization flow, and autosave framed as taking two minutes. Alternative: these are ordinary data conveniences enabled by the bank connection.
