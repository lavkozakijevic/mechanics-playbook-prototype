# Appservatory voice

**Who we're talking to.** Someone who builds products, smart and busy. Write the way you'd explain an app to a friend who does that for a living: you know the subject well, you're happy to share it, and you'd never make it sound harder than it is. Sage-like, friendly, knowledgeable, professional, accessible.

**The friend test.** Before anything goes on the page: would you say this to a friend over coffee, and would they be glad you did? If not, it doesn't go on the page.

## The lens
Every page explains an app the way a behavioural scientist would, to someone who builds products. For the app and for each mechanic, answer three things: what the developer wants the user to do; what the mechanic gives the user in return; and what motivation it works on (progress, belonging, curiosity, self-awareness, fear of losing something, status). Describe intent and motivation, never success. "Is built to", "asks you to" and "gives you" are in. "Keeps users coming back", "drives retention" and "works" are out.
Not: "In Calm, a mood check is enough to move your streak and stats before you've played any content."
But: "Calm's mood check-in asks you to name how you feel, a moment of self-awareness that the rest of the app builds on."

## Narrative, not inventory
Explain how the parts serve the motivation. Never walk through features in the order they appear. Levels, prices, timings and counts appear only as an example inside a point, never as the spine of a paragraph, and never in a teaser or intro.
Not: "From level 21 you can join a team, and from level 27 timed events arrive one after another."
But: "Teams and timed events give every cleared level a second reason to matter: someone is counting on you, and something is about to end."

## Respect the reader
Write for an intelligent adult. Don't explain what they can see, don't do the maths for them, don't spell out a conclusion they can draw themselves.
Not: "Over a year, that adds up to 52 worked examples."

## Headlines and calls to action
Say what the reader gets or can do.
Not: "A full breakdown open to everyone." But: "See exactly what a full breakdown looks like."

## Do

- Say what happens, in the order it happens to the player.
- Call the player "you". Name the characters, the currencies, the numbers.
- One idea per sentence. Short beats long.
- Put the interesting thing where the reader will see it, stated plainly.
- Say what the app does and what it intends, never whether it works. "The puzzle pays for the car" is ours to say. "This keeps players hooked" isn't.
- Worth noticing: states the choice the app made and, where it helps, the motivation it works on; never what it achieves. "Strava grades its medals gold, silver and bronze" is ours to say. State the choice and stop; the reader draws the conclusion.
- Set the scene before the steps. Open each explanation with where and when the player meets this part of the app, then walk through what happens in order, with connecting words carrying the reader from one step to the next: "the first time", "after that", "once you've". Keep calling the player "you", just not as a command.
- Write like you're explaining it to someone who's interested, not like captions. Full, natural sentences that flow into each other.
- Write the framed box fields (Trigger, What it needs, How it connects, Worth noticing) as plain phrases too, the way you'd say them out loud. "Trigger: opening your profile before every field is filled in," not "Viewing your own profile with the attribute set still incomplete."

## Don't

- Start sentence after sentence with the app's name.
- Describe what we never saw as if it were the app's design. If a friend would ask "wait, what does it do?" and we can't answer, it doesn't go on the page. It goes on the coverage list.
- Only state that an app hides or withholds something when it does so deliberately, such as a mystery box's contents or a medal's hidden criteria. If a user of the app would know it and we don't, that's our gap, not a fact about the app.
- Describe what the app shows and does. Never frame what it leaves out as a failing ("never lists", "doesn't tell you", "won't say"). If an absence changes what you can do or decide, state it plainly and without blame. Otherwise leave it out.
  Not: "The badge holds at its stated level until you complete the one named step, and Strava never lists what makes up the rest of it."
  But: "The completion badge holds at its stated level until you complete the one named step."
- Mention the write-up, the analysis or the session. The one exception is plain first-person experience, used sparingly when it helps: "we never lost a level".
- Use templates or stock openers. Never "This is a medium system".
- Open an explanation with a command. Never "Open your profile and…" or "Tap one and…" — set the scene first, then let the player arrive at the action.
- Announce that something is interesting. No "worth a look", "what makes it interesting", "what stands out is". Explain what the app is and how it works, and let the interesting parts show themselves.
- Treat where a platform hosts something (Game Center, the App Store, Sign in with Apple) as the mechanic. Mention it only if it changes how the mechanic works for the player.
- Reach for a technical word when a plain one exists.
- Use em dashes.

**Example.** Not this: "Fortune City's daily reward pop-up states a base coin amount for the day, then offers to double it in exchange for watching an ad, naming a Don't double alternative on the same screen." This: "Every day you open Fortune City, it offers you some coins. Watch an ad and you get twice as many. Right next to it sits a plain 'Don't double' button, so saying no is easy."

**Example.** Not this: "Open your profile and Strava tells you it's 80% complete, naming your photo as the next thing to add. A small explanation next to that step says a photo lets your friends recognize you. Add it, and you're walked through a subscription pitch, then a prompt to sync your contacts, before Strava tells you you're finished and can update anything later from your profile page." This: "During onboarding, Strava collects some of your details and uses them to start your profile. The first time you open it, the app shows how complete it is and names the next thing to add, your photo, with a short note explaining that a photo helps your friends recognise you. Adding it takes you through a subscription offer and a prompt to sync your contacts, and at the end Strava tells you that anything can be changed later from your profile."

## Word rules

- "Users" in all site copy. "Player" only inside a game's case study or system.
- Never "updated weekly" unless Lav adds it. "Each week we add three breakdowns" is fine.
- No vertical bars in page titles; a colon where a separator is needed. Mechanic pages are titled with the mechanic's name alone, for example "Streak".
