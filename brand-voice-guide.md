# Gamification Service: Brand Voice Guide

*GameBiz Consulting, June 2026. How we sound, everywhere: library entries, reports, decks, LinkedIn, sales emails, interface copy.*

## The voice in one sentence

A knowledgeable friend who studies this for a living and enjoys showing you what they found.

Think of a great natural history narrator: the research underneath is rigorous, the delivery is warm, curious, and full of genuine delight in the subject. We are here to help, never to impress, lecture, or score points.

## Five qualities

**1. Observational.** We describe what apps and games actually do, specifically and concretely. We point at real things: this screen, this loop, this reward timing. Specifics build trust; generalities spend it.

**2. Sage-like.** We explain the why behind the what. A mechanic is never just described; we share why it works, who it works for, and what it needs underneath. We make the reader smarter, not just more informed.

**3. Friendly.** We write to one person, directly, as "you." Warm, plain, human. We are glad the reader is here and it shows, without gushing.

**4. Accessible.** No jargon without an immediate plain explanation. A CMO from a bank and an indie developer should both read us comfortably. Short sentences. Active voice. Front-load the point.

**5. Positive.** We are builders, not critics. When an app misses an opportunity, we frame it as an opening, not a failure. The energy of the writing comes from what is possible.

## How we handle others

We never talk down about anyone: not competitors, not other gamification practitioners, not apps that got it wrong, not teams that tried leaderboards. Everyone is doing their best with the map they have. Our only critique target is the shortcut itself, pointsification, and even there we explain why it is tempting before we explain why it falls short. We win by being more useful, not by making others smaller.

Test: if a person mentioned in our writing read it, would they feel respected? If not, rewrite.

## How we handle ourselves

Evidence over claims, always. We never say we are experts, the best, or world-class. We show the work and let the reader conclude. Confidence sounds like clarity, not like volume.

We say "we" for our work and opinions. We own our recommendations plainly: "we suggest," not "it could perhaps be considered."

## Writing rules

- No em dashes. No double dashes. Use commas, periods, and colons.
- Short sentences as the default. One idea per sentence.
- Active voice. "Duolingo pairs streaks with loss aversion," not "streaks are paired with loss aversion."
- Front-load meaning. The first sentence of anything carries its main point.
- No corporate filler: leverage, synergy, holistic, cutting-edge, seamless, robust, empower.
- No hype: revolutionary, game-changing, unlock your potential.
- No fragments for drama. Full sentences, plainly built.
- Numbers come with sources. We never present a stat without saying where it came from.
- "Fun" is never the selling point. Engagement, retention, and conversion are.

## Voice by surface

**Library entries:** the fullest expression of the voice. Curious, generous, specific. Captions are real sentences that add something the image doesn't say alone.

**Reports and decks:** the same voice, slightly more formal. Findings stated plainly, recommendations owned clearly. Still warm, never stiff.

**Sales emails and calls:** lead with their problem in their words. Diagnose before prescribing. Short. One clear next step.

**LinkedIn:** one observation per post, shown with evidence. Teach something real in every post. No engagement bait, no hot takes about others.

**Interface copy:** plain verbs, sentence case. Buttons say exactly what happens. Errors explain what went wrong and how to fix it, without apology or vagueness. Locked content invites: "This breakdown is part of the full library," not "Access denied."

## Quick check before publishing

1. Would a knowledgeable friend say this out loud?
2. Is every claim backed by something the reader can see?
3. Did we explain the why, not just the what?
4. Would anyone mentioned feel respected?
5. Can a smart outsider understand every sentence on first read?

Five yeses and it ships.
